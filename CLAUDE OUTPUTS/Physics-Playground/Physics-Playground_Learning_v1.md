# Learning: building a no-math physics playground

Over coffee, here is what happened and why.

## Step 1: The approach, and why

The brief was "highly interactive and graphical physics lessons, lowest level, no math." My starting point was a question, not a topic list: what does a lesson look like if you are not allowed to write a single equation? The answer I landed on is that the equation has to be *inside the picture*. You cannot say "friction decelerates the ball." You can let the person flick a ball on ice and then on carpet and feel the difference in their thumb.

So each lesson got the same spine, in this order: a picture that sets the scene, one big idea in one sentence, a live model you poke, a short "try this" list that steers the poking toward the point, a plain-words "what you saw" that names what happened, and one tap-to-answer check. The check is there because reading "the feather floats because of air" is not the same as having predicted it.

The ten topics follow the usual order of an intro physics course (motion, forces, gravity, energy, collisions, heat, waves, light, magnetism, buoyancy), but each one is phrased as something a kid already half-knows: things keep going, pushes and pulls, everything falls together, hot is jiggly. That order is not arbitrary. Forces need motion first. Energy needs forces. Collisions need both. Heat is where "jiggle" gets introduced, and sound reuses it.

## Step 2: Roads not taken

- **A slide deck with pictures and animations.** Rejected first. You can illustrate inertia, but you cannot *feel* that the ball on carpet stops sooner from a slide. Interactivity is the whole lesson here, not a garnish.
- **One big sandbox with everything in it.** Tempting, and it is how a lot of physics toys work. Rejected because a beginner with no map just makes a mess. Ten small sandboxes, each rigged so that one idea is the obvious thing to discover, teach more than one open world.
- **Hidden numbers you can reveal.** I considered a "show the math" toggle on every sim. Dropped it. The brief said no math, and a toggle invites the reader to feel they are missing something. The numbers can come in a second set of lessons once the intuitions exist.
- **A physics engine library (Matter.js).** Would have made collisions and stacking easy. Rejected because every sim here is one-dimensional or nearly so, and a library would have added a dependency and made the "why does this behave this way" less legible in the source. Hand-written rules also let me cheat on purpose (see Step 5).
- **Voice narration.** Good for the youngest learners. Parked: it needs recorded audio, which is a production job, not a build job.

## Step 3: How the pieces connect

The page is one file with one data structure: an array of ten lesson objects. Each object holds the words (title, big idea, try-this list, what-you-saw, quiz) and a function that builds the sim. A single runner handles everything the sims share: sizing the canvas to the phone, scaling for high-resolution screens, routing pointer events, the animation loop, and reading the theme colors so every sim looks right in light and dark mode.

That split matters. The words and the physics live in different places, so a sentence can be rewritten without touching a simulation, and a simulation can be tuned without re-reading the prose. The "try this" list is written against the controls that exist in that sim. If a control changes, the list is the first thing to re-check.

Progress is saved on the device with the browser's local storage. Lessons are addressed by a hash in the link (`#magnets`), so a specific lesson can be shared.

The eleven pictures were generated by Google Gemini's image model (Nano Banana) from one shared style sentence plus one scene sentence each. The style sentence is what keeps them looking like a set.

## Step 4: Tools, methods, frameworks

- **HTML Canvas, hand-written.** Every sim is a few dozen lines of "each frame, move things a little by these rules." For teaching, the rules are the content, so I wanted them readable.
- **Real physics where it is cheap, fake physics where it is clearer.** Collisions use the actual elastic-collision rule, because it is three lines and the surprising behaviors (small ball bounces back, equal balls swap) fall out of it for free. Magnets use a toy model (two poles per bar, pull or push that fades with distance) because the real thing is much harder and looks the same at this level.
- **Predict-observe-explain** is the teaching pattern. "Try this" is the prediction prompt, the sim is the observation, "what you saw" is the explanation. This is the standard structure in physics education research (White & Gunstone, 1992, *Probing Understanding*), and it beats explain-first because the surprise is what makes the idea stick.
- **PhET as the reference point.** The University of Colorado's PhET simulations are the gold standard for this genre. The design rule I borrowed from them is "implicit scaffolding": the controls themselves limit what you can do, so the thing you discover is the thing you were meant to discover.
- **Playwright** for a headless check: it walked all ten lessons, clicked the primary button in each, answered a quiz, and reported no script errors and no sideways scroll at phone width. One contact sheet of screenshots caught a visible bug (a stray "null" rendered as text) before you saw it.

If I had picked a physics library instead, the sims would be more realistic and less legible, and I could not have made the Moon fall slower with one number.

## Step 5: Tradeoffs

- **Legibility over realism.** The feather on Earth falls at a fixed slow speed and sways. Real feather aerodynamics are chaotic. The fake one teaches the same point faster.
- **Ten shallow lessons over four deep ones.** Each sim does one thing. You can exhaust a lesson in two minutes. That is the cost of being clear to a total beginner.
- **One file over an app.** Works on a phone tonight, no install. Loses sound on devices where the page has not been tapped yet (browsers require a tap before audio), so the sound lesson says "turn your sound on" and the string is silent until you pluck.
- **Generated illustrations over diagrams.** They set a mood and make the page inviting. They do not teach anything; the canvas does. If the budget were tight, the pictures are the first thing to cut.

## Step 6: Mistakes and dead ends

1. **The "null" bug.** On every lesson except the first, the word "null" appeared as text at the top. I used a conditional that returned null for the intro block and passed it to the browser's own append function, which happily prints "null." My helper function skips nulls, but the native one does not. A screenshot caught it. Lesson: when you have two ways to do the same thing, you will eventually mix them up.
2. **Liquids that behaved like gases.** In the heat sim, I added a gentle downward pull so liquid dots would settle, then overwrote it every frame by normalizing their speed. The dots filled the cup evenly. Fixed by simply fencing liquid dots into the bottom half of the cup. A crude rule that looks right beats an elegant rule that does not.
3. **The energy ball could be dragged into the wall.** The track starts almost vertical at the left edge, so a drag to the far left parked the ball in a corner. Narrowed the drag range.
4. **A sandbox safety check blocked one of my own commands.** It was a long shell command with embedded files and the checker could not parse it. No data was at risk. I split the work into smaller steps. Lesson: long one-shot commands are brittle in more ways than one.

## Step 7: Pitfalls for next time

- **Touch first.** A sim that needs a mouse hover is broken on a phone. Everything here works with drag and tap only.
- **The caption is the teacher.** Each sim writes a sentence describing what is happening right now ("Opposite ends face each other. The loose magnet is being pulled in"). Without it, a learner sees shapes moving and does not know what to notice. Budget as much time for the captions as for the physics.
- **Clamp everything.** Every draggable thing needs a fence. Every speed needs a cap. Learners will immediately try to break the toy, and a ball that flies off the canvas teaches nothing.
- **Dark mode is not an afterthought.** Canvas does not inherit page colors. The sims read the theme's color tokens each frame, which is why they still look right when the phone is in dark mode.
- **Generated images need a shared style sentence.** Eleven separate prompts produce eleven styles. One appended style clause produced a set.

## Step 8: What an expert sees that a beginner misses

A beginner evaluates a physics sim by how realistic it looks. An expert evaluates it by whether the *wrong* intuition is visibly contradicted. The collision sim is built so the big-ball-into-small-ball result (big one barely slows) contradicts the common belief that "hitting something always stops you." The gravity sim exists to contradict "heavier falls faster." If a sim only confirms what people already think, it is decoration.

The second thing an expert notices: every sim has exactly one surprise, and the controls make that surprise hard to miss. That is a design choice, not a limitation.

## Step 9: Lessons for completely different projects

- **If you cannot use the usual language, put the idea in the object.** No math meant the physics had to be in the behavior of the thing on screen. The same move works for a product demo that cannot use jargon or a report for a reader who will not read: make the thing itself carry the point.
- **Prediction before explanation.** Ask the reader what they think will happen before showing them. It works for teaching, for pitching, and for writing. The gap between the guess and the result is where attention lives.
- **A fixed template across ten items is a feature.** Same spine every lesson. The reader learns the shape once and then only spends attention on content. Newsletters, decks and onboarding flows all benefit from the same discipline.
- **Fake it where the fake teaches the same thing.** The Moon in this page is just "fall slower." That is enough. Knowing which corners can be cut without changing the lesson is most of expertise.

## Epistemic check

The physics in the "what you saw" texts is textbook-level and uncontroversial (Newton's first and second laws, equivalence of gravitational acceleration, energy conservation, elastic collisions, kinetic theory of heat, pitch versus loudness, rectilinear propagation of light, magnetic poles, Archimedes' principle). The one historical claim, Dave Scott's hammer-and-feather drop on Apollo 15 in 1971, is well documented by NASA. I did not triangulate with Gemini or ChatGPT on this run: the content is introductory and the risk is in simplification, not fact. The simplifications worth knowing about are listed under Step 5.
