What is happening

Every slab is a thin prism: a flat cross-section of area A(xᵢ) extruded by the thickness Δx = (b − a)/N. Its volume is A(xᵢ)·Δx, so the whole stack is a Riemann sum of A(x). The exact volume is the limit of that sum:

V = limN→∞ Σ A(xᵢ) Δx = ∫ab A(x) dx,  with A(x) = c · f(x)²

The constant c depends only on the cross-section's shape: π for a disk, 4 for a square of side 2f, √3 for an equilateral triangle of side 2f, π/2 for a semicircle. Change the shape and every volume scales by c, which is Cavalieri's principle in action: equal-area slices at every height mean equal volumes.

The lower chart plots A(x). Each rectangle's area is literally one slab's volume, so the stack volume is the rectangle total and the exact volume is the area under the curve.

Things to try

  • Cylinder. Exact at N = 1. Slicing only adds work when the cross-section changes along the axis.
  • Paraboloid with midpoint slabs. Also exact at every N, because A(x) = πx is linear and the midpoint rectangle matches a linear function perfectly.
  • Cone, inscribed versus circumscribed. The two stacks squeeze the answer from both sides. This is the structure of Archimedes' and Eudoxus' exhaustion arguments, two millennia before limits were defined.
  • Sphere, square cross-sections. The volume becomes 16/3, no π anywhere, even though the base is a circle.
  • Error chart, cone or horn. Edge rules fall like 1/N (slope −1). The midpoint rule falls like 1/N² (slope −2). Doubling N buys one more decimal place with edge slabs, two with midpoint slabs.
  • Error chart, sphere or bell. Now the edge rules also fall like 1/N². The solid is symmetric, so the left-edge overshoot on one half cancels the undershoot on the other. Symmetry is free accuracy.
  • Vase, any rule, N ≥ 3. Exact to machine precision. A(x) is a sum of sines and cosines over one full period, and equally spaced samples of such a function add up exactly once N exceeds the highest frequency present. The chart drops those points because there is nothing left to plot.
  • Horn, N = 1, left edge. One fat slab with volume 3π for a solid of 0.75π. A single sample can be wildly wrong when the function changes fast.

Where the picture misleads

  • Volume converges; surface area need not. The stack's volume approaches the true volume, but the stack's surface area does not approach the true surface area. The staircase edges never flatten out. That is why arc length and surface area need a different setup (frustums, not cylinders).
  • "Thinner and thinner" is not "infinitely many infinitely thin." No single slab ever has zero thickness. The exact volume is a limit, a statement about where the sums are heading, not a sum of zero-volume sheets.
  • The 1/N² rate needs smoothness. It relies on A having a bounded second derivative. The sphere's f(x) = √(1−x²) has an infinite slope at the ends, but A = π(1−x²) is a smooth parabola, which is why the sphere still converges cleanly. A solid whose A(x) has a kink or jump converges more slowly.
  • Any slicing direction works, but not equally well. Slicing the same solid along a different axis gives a different A(x) and a different error at the same N. The limit is the same; the road there is not.
  • Pulling the slabs apart changes nothing numerically. The gap slider only spreads the picture so the pieces are visible. The volumes are identical.
