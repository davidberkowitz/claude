Triangle Limit

Fill a circle with N triangles that meet at the center. Count their area, unroll them into a strip, and watch the total close in on πr² as the triangles get thinner. This is Archimedes' route to π, and it is the two-dimensional cousin of slicing a solid.

What is happening

Each triangle has its apex at the center, a base of length b, and a height h measured from the center to the base. Its area is ½·b·h. All N triangles share the same h, so the total is

A = N · ½ · b · h = ½ · (N·b) · h = ½ · perimeter · h

As N grows, the perimeter N·b tends to the circumference 2πr and the height h tends to the radius r. So the area tends to ½ · 2πr · r = πr². The unrolled strip shows the same thing with no algebra: N triangles alternating up and down form a parallelogram of base ½·perimeter and height h, and that parallelogram straightens into a rectangle πr by r.

Inscribed triangles have their corners on the circle, so b = 2r·sin(π/N) and h = r·cos(π/N). Circumscribed triangles have their bases tangent to the circle, so b = 2r·tan(π/N) and h = r. One total is always below πr², the other always above, and the circle is trapped between them.

Things to try

  • N = 6, inscribed. Six equilateral triangles. The total is 3√3/2 ≈ 2.598, which is why a hexagon looks like a crude circle but is 17% short on area.
  • N = 96, both. This is Archimedes' polygon count. His bounds were 3 10/71 < π < 3 1/7, taken from perimeters rather than areas. The circumscribed area here equals half the circumscribed perimeter, so it matches his upper bound. The inscribed area sits a little below his lower bound, because an inscribed polygon's area is ½ · perimeter · h with h < r.
  • Error chart. Both the inscribed and circumscribed errors fall like 1/N². The circumscribed error is half the size of the inscribed one at every N, which is visible as a constant gap between the two lines.
  • The blend line. One part inscribed plus two parts circumscribed, divided by three, cancels the 1/N² term, and the error drops like 1/N⁴. With N = 96 that blend gives π to six decimals. Archimedes had the numbers to do this and did not; the idea had to wait two thousand years for Richardson.
  • Pull the triangles apart. The gaps make the count visible. The areas do not change.

Where the picture misleads

  • Here the perimeter converges too, unlike the slab stack. In Slab Limit the stack's volume converged but its surface area did not. Here the polygon's perimeter does converge to the circumference. The difference: a chord's direction approaches the tangent direction as it shrinks, while a staircase step never tilts toward the diagonal. Convergence of lengths needs the directions to converge, not just the positions.
  • No triangle ever fills the circle. The curved cap outside each inscribed triangle never disappears; it only gets thinner. πr² is the limit of the totals, not the total of infinitely many triangles.
  • This is not a proof that π exists. The argument shows area = ½ · circumference · r. Defining π as circumference over diameter is a separate step, and proving that ratio is the same for every circle (Euclid XII.2) is another.
  • The π estimate is only as good as the sine. The page uses the computer's sin and tan, which already know π. Archimedes could not, and computed the side lengths by repeated square roots from the hexagon. The honest version of this page is the one in his head.
  • 1/N² is slow. Each extra correct digit of π costs about three times as many triangles. Modern π computations use series and iterations that gain digits exponentially, not polygons.
